Real, fake, and the illusion of simplicity in this week’s retro circuit

A matchbook from Infocom’s The Witness turns out to be genuinely Chinese, not kitsch; 90s arcades worked their adult veneer and secret economies in different directions; one fan finally gets a TradeWars 2002 stand‑in that refuses modern bloat while Sega’s bass sequel swerves into sim; Llamasoft’s moose flies its own flag.

The week’s most satisfying twist wasn’t in a game; it was on a matchbook. While prepping Infocom’s The Witness for his Visible Zorker app, one writer fixated on the circular “Chinese” symbol on the Brass Lantern matchbook—surely a made‑up glyph to go with the Chandler/Ramanujan gag phone number and punny restaurant name. It wasn’t. After asking around, he learned it’s the shou character for long life, the sort of decorative lettering that reads instantly if you’ve seen thousands of variants and barely at all if you haven’t. He even jokes the package designers might’ve made it too readable. Andrew Plotkin on Zarf Updates

Once you start looking for staged authenticity, Sega’s The House of the Dead reads like a thesis. The team ditched Virtua Cop’s lock‑on ballet for enemies who shamble into your face, then amped the problem by making them survive multiple shots—so many test players thought the game was “wrong” because targets didn’t drop on contact. No visible timer, just barrels and axes to intercept while your reloads and precision lag behind the tide. Blood could even be flipped to green at the operator menu, which says a lot about its “for adults” posture being both sincere and configurable. Marc on Retro XP

And then it goes tender: whether you clip headshots and rescue scientists doesn’t just adjust routes (miss the bridge save and you’re headed underground), it really does decide if Sophie lives, dies, or lurches back dead-eyed because you needed a continue. Score 62,000+ without a continue for the good ending; bring fewer receipts and the mansion’s final beat curdles. It’s arcade cruelty framed as story consequence, years before achievements were invented to codify the same itch. Marc on Retro XP

Mortal Kombat II took that same cabinet-era need to matter and carved it into the select screen. Shang Tsung drops from final boss to playable troll while Kano and Sonya are literally chained offstage, a wordless power diagram that tells you exactly what Shao Khan can do. Secrets grow teeth: Jade and Smoke peeking from the Living Forest, Noob Saibot as a 50‑wins specter, the Kombat Tomb’s ceiling spikes only waking up for a stage fatality, the Dead Pool’s acid being, well, acid. Also, the arena crowd only cheers when the boss hits you, which is deliciously petty. Kimimi on Kimimi the Game Eating She-Monster

MKII also trusts you to enjoy the bit, not just the blood. Friendships and Babalities require deliberate restraint—win without punching in the last round—so the joke doubles as a flex. The result, as one critic argues, is a highlight: the fighting is merely OK, but the world’s rumor economy and visual winks make the game feel lived in rather than explained. Kimimi on Kimimi the Game Eating She-Monster

On the other side of the cabinet, one old BBS door game finally gets the clone it deserves. Warp to Sector One is a straight‑laced TradeWars 2002 stand‑in: three commodities (ore, organics, equipment), stations to buy fighters and pick up colonists, planet archetypes like mining colonies and fortresses, click‑to‑move with a command line for purists, and skeuomorphic CRT options if you want to role‑play phosphor burn‑in. “Vapid by modern standards,” the post admits—but that’s the point, and the relief, after decades of bloated space traders. Scopique on Scopique

Sega’s fishing series heads the opposite direction. Get Bass 2 reins in the arcade breathlessness of its predecessor and leans sim: time of day and weather, gentle boat puttering, fish that often refuse to bite, a catch mechanic that gives them one last squirming chance to escape, and tournaments where five fish can win the whole day. The sequel even lets pike waste your time during a bass event. The writer prefers the first game’s sparkle but respects a sequel that chooses a different identity instead of “The First One: Take Two.” Kimimi on Kimimi the Game Eating She-Monster

Meanwhile, Llamasoft’s Moose Life is classic Minter that refuses to be a cover song. You fly a voxel moose through neon tunnels, flipping to the ceiling to shoot as needed; it’s harder than Polybius but comes with a Free Ride mode, and it quietly saves best-state progress so you’ll actually see late levels. If there’s a direct inspiration, it isn’t obvious—maybe a whiff of Defender or Juno First, but mostly it’s its own oddball. The post even points toward the Heart of Neon doc if you want the longer arc. Michael on Virtual Moose

If all of that toggling between surface and substance sounds familiar, a visual novel reader spent a whole entry wrestling with the same thing: in Umineko, only the red truths and what Battler sees deserve trust; everything else can be theater. Magic shows up as concealment, a sleight-of-hand to delay revelation. She’s chewing on drift bottles and handwriting, too—when does a found message anchor reality, and when is it bait? It’s the same game MKII plays with ninjas in the trees, and the same wink hiding on an Infocom matchbook. Tofutush on Tofutush's Blog

So yes, this week you could chase purity and find it in a text‑mode trader dressed up like a terminal, or in a moose that explodes voxels without apologizing for being weird. You could wade back into 90s arcades where gore is an option, secrets are a lifestyle, and grown‑up vibes are a switch flip away. And you could remember that the paper we saved from the era was often telling the truth—we just didn’t know how to read it yet.
